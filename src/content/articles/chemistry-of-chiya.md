There are two kinds of people: those who add milk to tea, and those who add tea to milk. In Nepal this argument happens in every kitchen, every day, forever. ATOMS decided to settle it the only way we know how — with controlled experiments and way too much caffeine.

## The question

Does the order of adding milk change the tea? And if so — why?

## The setup

Fourteen batches. Same brand of tea leaves, same stove, same pot, same amount of milk from the same carton. The only variable: when the milk went in.

| Batch | Method |
|---|---|
| A | Water → leaves → boil → milk added at the end |
| B | Cold milk + water together → leaves → boil |

We measured brewing temperature over time, noted colour depth, and ran a blind taste test with nine volunteers (and one teacher who refused to leave).

## What happened

**Batch B — milk from the start — made noticeably smoother tea.** The blind test agreed: seven of nine tasters preferred it, mostly describing it as "less sharp" and "creamier without being milky."

The chemistry backs this up. Milk proteins — mostly casein — denature and can develop a slightly cooked, sulphurous taste when they hit liquid near boiling point. Pouring cold milk into already-boiling tea shocks the proteins hard. Heating them gently from the start, the way Batch B did, means fewer dramatic reactions and a softer flavour.

There's also a tannin angle: tea tannins bind to proteins. When milk is present during the boil, some tannins lock onto milk proteins instead of staying free in the brew — which literally means less bitterness reaching your tongue.

## The honest conclusion

Science requires us to admit the following: the difference is real, measurable, and small. If you have strong feelings about tea order, those feelings will overpower a blind taste test anyway. That's not a failure of the experiment — that's the finding.

The real result is this: your grandmother's method survived because it works. The chemistry agrees with her. It usually does.
