Every physics textbook has the same diagram: a particle shooting through a detector, leaving a neat little trail behind it. We looked at that diagram for months. Then someone asked the only question that matters — *can we see it for real?*

The answer turned out to be yes, for about the price of two plates of momo.

## What a cloud chamber actually is

A cloud chamber doesn't detect particles directly. It creates a layer of supersaturated alcohol vapour — cold enough that the air is holding more vapour than it should be able to. When a charged particle tears through that layer, it knocks electrons off the gas molecules it passes. The disturbed molecules become condensation sites, and the vapour condenses into a fine mist along the particle's exact path.

The particle writes its own signature in fog.

## The build

Our final version cost almost nothing:

1. **A transparent plastic box** — the chamber itself
2. **Dry ice** — sourced from the ice factory near Chabahil, to chill a black metal plate at the bottom
3. **Isopropyl alcohol** — soaked into a felt lining at the top, so it evaporates downward
4. **A flashlight and a black cloth** — because the trails are faint, and you need side-lighting against a dark background to catch them

The first attempt failed completely. So did the second. The plate wasn't cold enough, and the vapour layer never formed. The trick, we learned from an old CERN outreach video, is patience — the chamber needs twenty minutes to stabilize before anything happens.

## What we saw

At first, nothing. Then — thin white threads, appearing out of nothing, lasting half a second, and gone.

Most of what we saw were **muons**: heavy cousins of electrons, born when cosmic rays from dying stars collide with the upper atmosphere forty kilometres above our heads. Some of those particles were created before any of us were born, travelled the width of a planet, and ended their existence in a plastic box on the second floor of our school.

One trail was thick and jagged — probably an alpha particle from trace radioactive material in the air. One curved slightly, which we still argue about.

## What we learned

The physics was the easy part. The real lesson was that science has a waiting problem. Nothing about a cloud chamber is hard — but everything about it tests whether you actually care enough to sit still for twenty minutes in a cold lab, staring at nothing, on the off chance that the universe decides to show you something.

It usually does.
