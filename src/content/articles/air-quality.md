We always assumed the road outside would be the worst place to breathe. It wasn't.

For a week, two of us carried a pair of low-cost PM2.5 sensors we soldered ourselves — one near the main gate on Ring Road, one in the courtyard, one in the chemistry lab, one in a classroom with the windows closed. Same sensors, same calibration, logged every ten minutes to a phone.

## What the numbers said

The **chemistry lab** spiked hardest, but only during practicals — burning magnesium, heating copper sulfate. It cleared in twenty minutes with the windows open.

The **gate** was consistently bad from 7:30 to 9:00 — traffic rush. But the **classroom with closed windows** was worse for longer. CO₂ built up, and the PM2.5 from chalk dust + 32 people breathing in a closed box stayed high all morning.

The **courtyard** was the cleanest, even though it's open. Trees matter.

## What we changed

We made a one-page poster for each classroom: open windows for ten minutes every period, even in winter. Teachers actually did it. The next week's logs showed a 30% drop in the closed classroom's average.

It's not a paper. It's not publishable. But it's the first time a graph we made changed how people open a window.
